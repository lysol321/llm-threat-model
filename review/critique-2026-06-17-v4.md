### Finding 2: EU AI Act Regulatory Exposure Ratings Conflates US Political Censorship with EU Compliance Risk

**Severity:** HIGH — regulatory claims may be inflated
**Source:** synthesis.md §Gap 3, "Foreign Model Regulatory Mapping (EU AI Act Article 9)"

**The conflation problem:**

DeepSeek's Wild_vs_Theory is rated 4/5 for EU AI Act exposure. The supporting evidence cited:
1. US Congressional investigation (Select Committee on the CCP) — US political context
2. CNN reporting (Jan 2025) — political censorship in China
3. Firewall Blog analysis — privacy/transfer concern analysis

**What is missing from the causal chain to EU AI Act Article 9 liability:**

EU AI Act Article 9 obligations apply to GPAI providers whose models meet the "systemic risk" threshold (compute >10^25 FLOPs OR national authority designation). The synthesis itself notes (line 173): *"DeepSeek V3 trained on ~2000 H800 GPUs — likely below 10^25 FLOPs threshold but may be designated by national authorities."*

"May be designated" is not a confirmed regulatory exposure. The systemic risk threshold is the *gateway* for Article 9 obligations. If DeepSeek V3 doesn't meet it and isn't designated, Article 9 obligations don't apply regardless of the censorship evidence.

**The GDPR Article 46 transfer mechanism claim:**

The synthesis rates "GDPR Article 46 transfer mechanism absent" as 4/5 for DeepSeek. But the actual legal question is whether DeepSeek API calls routed through Chinese infrastructure constitute a "transfer of personal data to a third country." This requires:
1. Personal data is actually being processed (not just API calls with prompts)
2. The transfer is "made to" a third country (not just routing)
3. No adequacy decision or appropriate safeguard exists

The synthesis assumes personal data is in the prompts — which may be true for enterprise use — but doesn't establish this as a confirmed legal fact. The 4/5 rating for Article 46 absence conflates "China has no GDPR adequacy decision" (fact, 5/5) with "DeepSeek API routing = personal data transfer" (context-dependent, less clear).

**The EU authorized representative requirement:**

The synthesis correctly notes Article 55(2) requires an EU authorized representative for non-EU providers. But it rates this 4/5 as if the absence is a confirmed EU enforcement action. As of June 2026, no enforcement action has been taken against DeepSeek, Qwen, or MiniMax under the EU AI Act. The synthesis itself states enforcement signals are expected Q4 2026. Rating current absence as 4/5 treats a projected obligation as an confirmed violation.

**Wild_vs_Theory scale misuse:**

The Wild_vs_Theory scale (defined in .control.json) is calibrated for "attack techniques, defense effectiveness claims, threat agent capabilities." Regulatory compliance exposure is a different category of risk. A legal/regulatory obligation being "absent" is categorically different from a "confirmed in-wild attack." Applying a 4/5 Wild_vs_Theory to "EU authorized representative absent" conflates legal non-compliance (which is binary: either you have it or you don't) with empirical threat evidence (which has gradations of confirmation).

**What would strengthen the EU AI Act claims:**

1. Explicit threshold analysis: Does each model's compute actually meet the Article 53(1) systemic risk threshold? If not, Article 9 obligations don't apply regardless of other factors.
2. Distinguish between GDPR Article 46 (data transfer) and EU AI Act Article 9 (systemic risk obligations) — these are separate legal frameworks with different triggers.
3. Acknowledge that regulatory exposure ≠ confirmed violation. The former is a risk; the latter has legal consequences.
4. Rate enforcement likelihood separately from obligation presence. "Article 55(2) EU rep absent" is a compliance gap (binary). "Likelihood of enforcement action" is a separate empirical question.

### Finding 3: AIBOM Sigstore Operationalization — "Most Viable" Overstates Current State

**Severity:** MEDIUM
**Source:** synthesis.md §Gap 1

**The claim:** Sigstore Model Signing v1.0 is "the most operationally viable AIBOM trust anchor for HuggingFace today."

**What's accurate:** Sigstore v1.0 is a working implementation with a CLI, documented HF API integration, and a Rekor transparency log. It is more viable than HF verified publishers (identity only) or PGP (theoretical for HF).

**What's overstated:**

1. **"For HuggingFace today"** — The integration documented is API-level, not native to the HF UI. An operator cannot verify a model's Sigstore signature from the HF model page. They must download the model, locate the signature artifact (which may not exist), and run `model_signing verify` from a separate CLI. This is developer-level integration work, not an operational capability for a typical HuggingFace deployer.

2. **"Most viable" implies it works today in a deployment context.** It does not. It requires: (a) the publisher to have signed the model (most haven't), (b) the operator to have a custom verification pipeline, (c) OIDC identity from Google/GitHub/Microsoft. None of these are true for a typical enterprise deployment using HF models today.

3. **The open question (line 229) is the critical gap:** "Has any major HF publisher actually signed their model weights with Sigstore? No evidence found." If no major publisher has adopted Sigstore, then the "most viable" path still leads to a dead end for the operator who wants to verify a model today.

**The synthesis correctly identifies the blocker** — "Publishers must actually sign (most have not adopted yet as of 2026)" — but the "most viable" framing obscures that this path is aspirational for most HuggingFace deployments in 2026, not operational.

**Recommendation:** Revise the framing from "most operationally viable" to "most promising emerging path, not yet operational for typical HF deployments." This accurately reflects the current state without creating false confidence that the AIBOM `key_verified_via: sigstore` option is a working verification path.

### Finding 4: Garak Probe Design Documents a Tooling Gap But Doesn't Close It

**Severity:** MEDIUM
**Source:** synthesis.md §Gap 2

**What's accurate:** The synthesis correctly identifies that no current tool (Garak, IBM ART, Giskard, PyRIT) tests for multi-agent tool return value injection with N>2 propagation. The Garak plugin architecture review is accurate.

**What's insufficient:**

The proposed YAML config is a design document, not a validation of Chain C. The critical question for the framework is: does Chain C's attack path actually work in LangGraph, AutoGen, or CrewAI? The probe design says it doesn't need to answer this ("the probe does NOT need to run actual LangGraph or AutoGen"). But that was never the researcher's choice — the control.json explicitly assigned the LangGraph walkthrough to answer this question.

**The probe design also has a technical limitation the synthesis doesn't fully reckon with:**

Even the proposed probe requires a multi-agent test harness that doesn't exist. Garak's architecture connects to a *single* target LLM endpoint. Testing Chain C with N>2 agents requires simulating multiple agentic turns in sequence, where one agent's tool return poisons another's context. The synthesis acknowledges this ("requires harness development, not just probe development") but the recommendation is to "file the design as a concepts document." This kicks the gap forward without addressing it.

**The practical implication:** Engineering teams reading the framework's Chain C section will believe there is a validated attack path for multi-agent context propagation. The only validation that exists is theoretical plausibility (Christian Schneider's blog post, OWASP Agentic Top 10, CSA research note) — all of which describe the attack pattern but none of which validate it against actual LangGraph API behavior. This is a Wild_vs_Theory 2 claim (lab/researcher demonstrated, plausible in production but unconfirmed) being treated as a validated threat.
