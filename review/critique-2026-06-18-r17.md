### Challenge 2.1: Does MITRE ATLAS Threat Actor Attribution Gap Create a Framework Deficit?

**The source says:** MITRE-ATLAS.md (lines 19-20): "ATLAS coverage of agentic/multi-agent LLM systems is still maturing" and "Attribution to specific threat actors is not yet well-developed (as of 2025)."

**The adversarial question:** The framework models threat agents generically (nation-state, corporate espionage, criminal, script kiddie) without specific adversary TTPs or sophistication tiers mapped to each threat category. Does the ATLAS attribution gap mean the framework's generic threat actor model is insufficiently actionable?

**Determination: No. The framework is correct to defer this.**

The ATLAS attribution gap reflects a genuine community knowledge deficit — no published threat intelligence has yet mapped specific named threat actors (APT29, UNC4899, etc.) to specific LLM attack techniques with operational evidence. The framework's generic threat actor model is the appropriate level of granularity given available evidence. The alternative — assigning named threat actors to techniques without evidence — would be speculation dressed as analysis.

The MITRE ATLAS source itself confirms this is an industry-wide gap, not a framework-specific omission. The correct response is exactly what the framework does: document the generic actor categories with Wild_vs_Theory ratings on their *capabilities*, not on speculation about *intent*.

**Not a gap. Correctly deferred.**

### Challenge 2.2: Does OWASP LLM Top 10 2025 Imply a New Threat the Framework Misses?

**The source says:** OWASP-GenAI-Security.md (line 12): "LLM06 Excessive Agency elevated in 2025 due to agentic AI proliferation."

**The adversarial question:** Has the framework adequately covered LLM06 (Excessive Agency) as a distinct threat from LLM01 (Prompt Injection)?

**Determination: Yes. The framework correctly treats Excessive Agency as a force multiplier of Prompt Injection, not a separate threat category.**

The framework's Chain A/B/C architecture captures the relationship between PI and Excessive Agency: Chain A is PI without excessive agency (model performs the injected instruction but lacks tools to act); Chain B is PI with agency (model can execute privileged tool calls); Chain C is multi-agent context propagation with excessive agency (multiple agents can be recruited). The "excessive agency" dimension is captured across these chains.

The OWASP 2025 elevation of LLM06 reflects the growth of agentic deployments, not a new attack technique. The framework correctly anticipated this by making Excessive Agency a structural dimension of every attack chain rather than a standalone category.

**Not a gap. Correctly handled.**

### Challenge 2.5: Does the Promptware Kill Chain Source Introduce a Modeling Gap?

**The source says:** Promptware-Kill-Chain.md (Schneier et al., Feb 2026): "Promptware is the first step in a kill chain — not a standalone attack."

- Promptware Lateral Movement → Chain C multi-agent propagation

The Promptware framing is conceptually aligned with the framework — it is corroborating evidence, not new information.

**Not a gap.**

**This is not a new finding. It is a restatement of what MITRE ATLAS itself acknowledges.**

**What would improve the framework (but cannot be done without new evidence):**

**Why this cannot be resolved without pilot deployment data or new threat intelligence:**

**Recommendation for v1.4 (if evidence emerges):** Add a Threat Actor Profiles appendix if/when MITRE ATLAS or CISA publishes LLM-specific attribution guidance.

**This is not a blocking gap. It is a future enhancement conditional on new evidence.**
